## A. Simulation Setup

We consider a HAP at an altitude of 20 km and a UAV at a fixed altitude of 1 km. The synthetic horizontal HAP position is (11419.6551, 450) m. The UAV operates over a 1 km × 1 km region represented by 100 cell centers, with coordinates 50, 150, …, 950 m along each axis. The reference mission contains 30 slots of 20 s and has a maximum speed of 15 m/s, permitting transitions of at most 300 m per slot, including remaining stationary. The initial position is freely selected; deployment and return travel are excluded. These geometry and mission values are repository simulation assumptions, rather than measured deployment parameters.

All methods use the same saved clear-sky SKR map. P1 searches a finite catalog of 233 authenticated sources, comprising 197 Maxwell–Boltzmann (MB) and 36 uniform sources. It selects the MB parameters (ν, V_A) = (0.125, 8) at all 100 positions. Thus, changes among the positioning methods do not arise from changes in the selected source parameters. This is a catalog-restricted source choice, with V_A at the upper MB-catalog boundary; no continuous-source optimum or matched-energy shaping gain is inferred. The saved physical configuration uses a wavelength of 1550 nm, full divergence of 233 μrad, an 8-cm receiver diameter, reconciliation efficiency 0.95, channel excess noise 0.0186 SNU, and an ideal detector.

Clouds completely block the affected links. Their saved Gaussian-copula model has spatial covariance exp(−d/ell) and a stationary latent AR(1) process with coefficient ρ. Unless varied, the blockage probability, spatial length, and temporal coefficient are p = 0.4, ell = 250 m, and ρ = 0.9. Each configuration contains the same 100 mission IDs; common innovations support paired comparisons. The evaluated probability range is 0.1–0.8, and the temporal coefficients are 0, 0.3, 0.6, 0.9, and 0.98. Latent correlation parameters are distinct from binary blockage correlations.

Stationary (P2) selects the best fixed position using the entire saved mission. Greedy uses only current-slot states and the objective-consistent movement penalty. DP (P3) is the offline finite-grid benchmark. For each saved trajectory, we report mean usable SKR K, total travel distance D, and the mobility-penalized objective U = K − λD/1000. The representative penalty is λref = 9.468026677248516×10⁻⁶ bit/use. QKD unavailability is the fraction of selected slots with zero usable SKR, expressed as a percentage; no engineering threshold is applied. Means and pointwise confidence intervals use missions as independent units, rather than cells or slots. Existing bootstrap intervals are retained; missing tradeoff mean intervals are reconstructed from saved mission outputs and existing resampling counts. No source, cloud, channel, or trajectory optimization is rerun for this consolidation.

## B. Impact of Candidate UAV Positions

Figure 1 compares the three methods at λref for deterministic nested subsets of J = 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 original positions. The DP mean objective increases from 1.39004×10⁻⁴ to 1.54863×10⁻⁴ bit/use, an 11.41% increase. Over the same range, the stationary objective increases from 1.36348×10⁻⁴ to 1.50243×10⁻⁴ bit/use, while the Greedy objective increases from 1.07800×10⁻⁴ to 1.48311×10⁻⁴ bit/use. Adding positions expands the feasible set, so the optimal DP and stationary objectives cannot decrease. Greedy has no corresponding general monotonicity guarantee.

The J = 20 DP objective reaches 96.71% of its J = 100 value. This finite-range result suggests diminishing incremental improvements beyond the initial expansion, but it does not identify an economically optimal grid size. In particular, the J = 10 transition graph contains eight connected components and seven isolated positions, whereas the evaluated subsets are connected from J = 20 onward. The initial improvement therefore reflects both expanded spatial choices and changes in connectivity, rather than candidate density alone.

## C. Impact of Cloud Blockage Probability

Figure 2(a) shows decreasing mean usable SKR with increasing p for all three methods. For DP, the mean falls from 1.57663×10⁻⁴ bit/use at p = 0.1 to 1.43475×10⁻⁴ bit/use at p = 0.8. Because the probability masks are nested under common innovations, increasing p removes usable rewards without changing the clear-sky map. This supports nonincreasing optimal objectives for P2 and DP; it does not impose a general ordering on availability or Greedy performance.

The deterioration in absolute performance is accompanied by a larger incremental benefit of mobility. The DP-minus-P2 objective difference increases from 1.11215×10⁻⁷ to 2.72806×10⁻⁵ bit/use, corresponding to relative improvements of 0.071% and 25.042%. At p = 0.8, the paired 95% interval for the absolute difference is [2.46760, 2.99896]×10⁻⁵ bit/use. Hence, the larger relative gain is accompanied by a larger absolute difference and is not solely a denominator effect. No interior maximum is observed on the evaluated probability grid, and no inference is made beyond p = 0.8.

Figure 2(b) shows that DP also reduces mean zero-SKR unavailability in this experiment. At p = 0.8, unavailability is 24.60% for P2, 16.53% for Greedy, and 4.07% for DP. This is an observed outcome of objective optimization, not proof that DP maximizes availability itself. Greedy also loses mean SKR as blockage increases, but its objective advantage over P2 is less consistent: its mean objective is below P2 at p = 0.1–0.4, and the paired differences at p = 0.5 and 0.7 have intervals containing zero. At p = 0.8, Greedy exceeds P2 by 8.54160×10⁻⁶ bit/use, compared with the larger DP improvement.

## D. Impact of Temporal Cloud Correlation

Figure 3 reports objective and travel distance at fixed p = 0.4, ell = 250 m, and λref. Objective is informative here because persistence changes both the available reward and the movement required to obtain it. As ρ increases from 0 to 0.98, the DP mean objective increases from 1.44559×10⁻⁴ to 1.56806×10⁻⁴ bit/use, while its mean travel distance decreases from 1038.25 to 31.84 m. The relative DP objective gain over P2 decreases from 23.712% to 0.155%.

Additional analysis of the saved masks and paths supports the hypothesis that persistence makes offline stationary selection more competitive. The cloud-free fraction at the selected P2 position increases from 77.03% to 99.97%. The mean number of positions remaining cloud-free throughout all 30 slots increases from 0 to 29.56, while the mean fraction of adjacent cell-slot states that change decreases from 48.21% to 6.35%. DP makes 7.36 position changes per mission at ρ = 0, compared with 0.19 at ρ = 0.98; the number of missions containing DP motion decreases from 100 to 10 out of 100.

These jointly observed changes are consistent with persistent favorable locations reducing the incremental value of movement. They do not isolate causality or establish a universal monotonic law. In particular, P2 and DP use future cloud states to select their initial positions, while Greedy does not look ahead. The latent correlation time −20/log(ρ) is approximately 990 s at ρ = 0.98, exceeding the 600-s mission. Empirical overall blockage also varies across the finite samples; it is reported separately rather than forced to equal the nominal probability.

## E. SKR–Mobility Tradeoff

Figure 4 connects saved policies in their original order across ten increasing penalties. The points describe evaluated SKR–distance operating choices; they are not claimed to form globally optimal physical Pareto frontiers. At λ = 0, DP achieves mean SKR 1.57305×10⁻⁴ bit/use with mean travel distance 1482.69 m. At λref, its distance decreases to 172.43 m, an 88.37% reduction, while mean SKR decreases by 0.515% to 1.56495×10⁻⁴ bit/use.

At λref, DP travels less than Greedy, whose mean distance is 430.61 m, while achieving higher mean SKR and objective. Relative to P2 and Greedy, the DP mean SKR improvements are 4.162% and 2.695%, respectively; the corresponding objective improvements are 3.075% and 4.418%. These quantities must be distinguished because the movement penalty appears only in U. At the tested penalties of 0.1λhi and above, all saved DP trajectories are stationary and coincide with P2 in objective. This identifies behavior at sampled penalties, rather than a continuous stopping threshold. Greedy can remain below P2 after becoming stationary because its initial position is selected from the first slot alone.

## F. Discussion and Limitations

Across the investigated ranges, the largest mean-SKR span is associated with candidate count for DP, speed for Greedy, and blockage probability for P2. These are descriptive ranges over different parameter domains, not normalized or universal sensitivity indices. Within the saved cloud sweeps, the largest relative DP-versus-P2 objective improvement occurs at p = 0.8, whereas the largest absolute difference occurs at ρ = 0. The finite spatial-correlation sweep also illustrates why relative and absolute effects should be reported together: increasing ell from 50 to 1000 m lowers absolute performance but increases the DP-versus-P2 objective difference from 2.25288×10⁻⁶ to 8.10831×10⁻⁶ bit/use. The fraction of slots with every candidate position blocked increases from 0 to 2.70%, directly limiting instantaneous spatial alternatives. The larger relative mobility gain therefore does not demonstrate increasing spatial diversity.

P1 establishes a common optimized clear-sky map, P2 captures offline selection of a fixed location, and DP−P2 measures the additional value of allowing motion within that offline setting. Since the selected source parameters remain identical across positions, cloud-avoidance gains cannot be attributed to changing source shaping. The three selected methods do not provide a matched-energy shaping ablation or isolate stationary-selection gain against a nonoptimized deployment. DP−Greedy also includes differences in future-state and initial-position information. Moreover, objective dominance does not imply higher unpenalized SKR in every mission at a positive penalty.

All conclusions are restricted to the finite source catalog, finite UAV grid, synthetic geometry and correlated complete-blockage model. The calculations omit link reacquisition, flight interruptions, deployment and return costs, and calibrated propulsion energy. The manuscript-style SKR evaluator combines two-dimensional mutual information with a homodyne Holevo expression and remains an unvalidated security model. Pointwise numerical confidence intervals do not establish quantum-security bounds. The reported results support numerical discussion under the recorded assumptions, rather than operational, finite-size, composable, or physical-security claims.
