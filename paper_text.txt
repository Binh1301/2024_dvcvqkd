Satellite-to-Ground Continuous Variable Quantum
Key Distribution: The Gaussian and Discrete
Modulated Protocols in Low Earth Orbit
Mikhael T. Sayat , Biveen Shajilal , Sebastian P. Kish , Syed M. Assad , Thomas Symul ,
Ping Koy Lam , Nicholas J. Rattenbury , and John E. Cater
Abstract— The
Gaussian
modulated
continuous
variable
quantum key distribution (GM-CVQKD) protocol is known to
maximise the mutual information between two parties during
quantum key distribution (QKD). An alternative modulation
scheme is the discrete modulated CVQKD (DM-CVQKD) pro-
tocol. In this paper, we perform a feasibility study for practical
satellite-to-ground CVQKD with a focus on comparing existing
GM-CVQKD and DM-CVQKD protocols with the application
of the performance of recent reconciliation methods. We use a
satellite-to-ground link model which takes into account geomet-
ric, scintillation, and scattering losses from the link distance,
atmospheric turbulence, and atmospheric aerosols, respectively.
In addition, recent multidimensional (MD) and multilevel coding
and multistage decoding (MLC-MSD) reconciliation method
models in combination with multiedge-type low-density parity-
check (MET-LDPC) code models have been used to determine
the reconciliation efficiency. Realistic parameters have been used
for the calculation of the secret key rate (SKR) along with a
comparison between MD and MLC-MSD reconciliation during
satellite-to-ground GM-CVQKD, for the first time. The results
show that GM-CVQKD outperforms DM-CVQKD. In addition,
GM-CVQKD with MD reconciliation outperforms GM-CVQKD
with MLC-MSD reconciliation in the finite size limit by producing
positive SKRs at larger link distances and lower elevation angles.
Index Terms— Quantum key distribution, continuous variable,
Gaussian modulation, discrete modulation, satellite communica-
tion, quantum communication.

advancement of QKD in space-based applications was the
demonstration of discrete variable QKD (DVQKD), which
uses the available degrees of freedom of single photons,
to encode a key between optical ground stations on Earth
and the Micius satellite [2]. Despite its successful deployment
in space, its use of expensive and inefficient single photon
detectors for detection poses challenges for its popularisa-
tion and commercialisation [3]. An appealing alternative is
continuous variable QKD (CVQKD) which uses multi-photon
technologies to encode the key in the continuous X and P
quadratures of light [3], [4]. Its use of homodyne or heterodyne
detection is more cost-effective, more compatible with stan-
dard telecommunication optical networks, and more efficient,
offering higher secret key rates (SKRs) [3], [5], [6].
CVQKD experiments have predominantly been restricted
to fibre-based systems in the laboratory where a SKR of
14.2 Mbit/s over 15 km of optical fibre has been demon-
strated using a local oscillator [7]. The first demonstration of
fibre-based CVQKD over 100 km was performed by control-
ling and suppressing excess noise [8]. A SKR of 2.1 Gbit/s
in free-space has been achieved using 10-channel wavelength
division multiplexing in the weak turbulence regime [9]
using the Kramers-Kronig scheme which digitally separates
the quadrature components [10], and a SKR greater than
1.68 Gbit/s can be reached in a lossless and excess noise
free system that uses two polarisations, six wavelengths,
and four orbital angular momentum for multiplexing [11].
In addition, an average SKR of 1.015 Mbit/s was achieved
over a turbulent 1 m free-space channel with a transmittance
between −6 dB to −15 dB using the passive-state-preparation
CVQKD protocol, which has the same security proof as
GM-CVQKD assuming Alice’s transmitter is trusted [12].
A study of atmospheric effects on quantum communications
over 1.6 km of free-space was performed and the experimental
setup was capable of CVQKD [13]. However, there has only
been one demonstration of free-space CVQKD which occurred
over 460 m in an urban environment and achieved a SKR of
0.152 kbit/s using unidimensional Stokes operator encoding
by modulating the quantum Stokes parameter with a Gaussian
distribution [14]. It should be noted that the Kramers-Kronig
scheme and Stokes operator encoding do not provide uncon-
ditional security, unlike GM-CVQKD [3].

The GM-CVQKD protocol is capable of achieving higher
SKRs as the coherent state distribution follows a continuous
Gaussian distribution allowing for more positions on the
optical phase space [3]. In contrast, DM-CVQKD positions a
finite number of coherent states on the optical phase space,

circumstances of perfect CVQKD and channel parameters,
GM-CVQKD produces larger SKRs. However, it is not evident
that DM-CVQKD performs better under realistic non-ideal
parameters. In fact, DM-CVQKD was proposed to address the
poor performance of CVQKD in the low SNR regimes [15].
Since the coherent states on the optical phase space follow
a discretised distribution, it may be argued that the states
are more distinguishable and can accommodate more excess
noise.
Previous studies on satellite-to-ground CVQKD provide a
variety of models for the laser propagation between a satellite
and an optical ground station (OGS) [21], [22], [23], [24], [25].
However, there are no published comparisons between existing
GM-CVQKD and DM-CVQKD protocols with the application
of the performance of existing reconciliation methods for

IV. SATELLITE-TO-GROUND CHANNEL MODEL
Clouds, atmospheric turbulence, and atmospheric aerosols
are the three main sources of signal degradation in the atmo-
sphere that cause the transmittance of the signal to decrease
through attenuation. The model developed considers the fol-
lowing: geometric losses due to the distance between Alice
(satellite) and Bob (OGS), the hardware used, scintillation
losses due to the atmospheric turbulence, scattering losses
due to atmospheric aerosols. Clouds are not included in the
model as they effectively destroy the signal and act as a
blockage, completely attenuating the signal. The solution to
this is accurate cloud coverage analyses and OGS network
site diversity [44] to spatiotemporally maximise channel links
between Alice and Bob regardless of environmental condi-
tions. In addition, weather conditions such as rain, snow, and
hail effectively destroy the signal and can cause damage to
an OGS telescope [45]. Therefore, in the presence of clouds,
rain, snow, and hail, satellite-to-ground CVQKD is not ideal.
Atmospheric

Several parameters were varied for the analysis of the

This section calculates the asymptotic limit SKRs for
satellite-to-ground GM-CVQKD and DM-CVQKD under col-

asymptotic limit SKRs are also compared to the loss-limited

The results in Figure 4 show that for a receiver aperture
diameter of 1 m and good atmospheric conditions, GM-

protocol does not produce any positive SKRs and is there-

smaller elevation angles produce smaller SKRs as the signal
travels through a larger link distance and effective atmosphere

outperforms the M-PSK protocol, where M-PSK can only

protocol can produce positive SKRs at small medium Earth

larger link distances.

• Operating in good atmospheric conditions where there

therefore satellite orbit altitude. This results from the
decrease in attenuation due to less atmospheric scattering
and turbulence.
• Protocols with a larger number of coherent states lead to
larger SKRs.
VI. DISCUSSION
The performance of recent MD and MLC-MSD recon-
ciliation methods have been applied to GM-CVQKD in a
satellite-to-ground context. It should be noted that the com-
plexities of these reconciliation methods differ leading to
different real-time decoding rates, and consequently, differ-
ent times taken for real-time secret key generation between
a satellite and OGS; the computational overhead of which
would be more of a burden on the satellite. This stems from
latency in data post-processing during the classical informa-
tion reconciliation step in QKD. In combination with power
consumption and heat dissipation in the satellite hardware,
these practical elements would lead to a decrease in the
achievable SKR. A practical solution to this would be to design
an appropriate thermal management system on the satellite
which would keep the satellite hardware to an appropriate low
temperature to mitigate the negative effects of heating [56].
To solve power consumption and latency issues, the use of
appropriate field-programmable gate arrays on the satellite
for data post-processing would lead to relatively lower power
consumption and high throughput by using their parallel
computational ability [57]. Overall, a CVQKD satellite would
need to be designed and engineered based on the desired SKRs
during CVQKD. An alternative solution would be to perform
information reconciliation offline and after the distribution of
the secret key. Given that a LEO satellite pass is typically
in the order of a few minutes, this is a promising solution for
satellite-to-ground based QKD where the final secret key (after
post-processing) is generated some time after the initial trans-
mission, over multiple passes of the satellite over the OGS.
High turbulence in the atmosphere causes beam wandering
during transmission between a satellite and an OGS. This
may result in a large beam angle-of-arrival on the receiver
aperture of the OGS, causing non-ideal coupling to fibre as
the beam may not be focused on the fibre, and therefore this
can introduce a probabilistic interruption in communication
between the satellite and OGS. The details of this phenomena
have previously been published [25], and it is similarly related
to the probability threshold, pthr, in Equation 31 which is the
probability that the received power is below the minimum
required power to register a signal, therefore causing an
interruption in communication and a substantial decrease in
achievable SKRs.
The loss-limited SKR upper bound was compared with
achievable asymptotic limit SKRs for GM-CVQKD and
DM-CVQKD. It should be noted that a thermal-loss SKR
upper bound, which incorporates noise components, and a
thermal-loss SKR lower bound have also been determined in
References [29] and [30]. However, as shown in Figure 3
in Ref. [29], the loss-limited SKR upper bound and the
thermal-loss SKR upper and lower bounds are identical in
LEO and low MEO altitudes. For this reason, the loss-limited
SKR upper bound was used for comparison. Furthermore, the
bounds are for ideal systems. In contrast, the SKRs calculated
for GM-CVQKD and DM-CVQKD consider parameters found
in practical satellite-to-ground CVQKD such as the reconcili-
ation efficiency as well as the modulation variance, which lead
to lower but more practical achievable SKRs.
VII. CONCLUSION
In this work, a feasibility study for practical satellite-
to-ground CVQKD with a focus on comparing existing
GM-CVQKD and DM-CVQKD protocols with the application
of the performance of recent reconciliation methods is
presented. The results show that GM-CVQKD outperforms
both the M-PSK and M-QAM DM-CVQKD protocols in the
asymptotic limit. However, the M-QAM protocol outperforms
the M-PSK protocol and is capable of producing positive
SKRs in both LEO and low MEO. In the finite size limit,
MD reconciliation outperforms MLC-MSD reconciliation by
producing larger positive SKRs in GM-CVQKD for larger link
distances and lower elevation angles. The physical implemen-
tations of both GM-CVQKD and DM-CVQKD as well as the
processes for MD and MLC-MSD reconciliation have not been
considered. These should be investigated further for future
satellite-to-ground and long distance CVQKD operation.
